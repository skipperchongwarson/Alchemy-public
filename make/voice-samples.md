# Make — voice samples

This is a reference file, not an instruction file. Instructions live in voice-and-writing.md and content-system.md. Before reaching for a hook template, check here first. Skipper's best work doesn't use templates — it opens mid-scene and lets the tension carry. Use these to calibrate tone, structure, and what "done" looks like across different content types.

## What these have in common
Every one opens in a scene or with a specific tension — not a topic introduction. Proof is always concrete (10 clients, 43%, $99 MRIs, none of them renewed). The ending either asks a real question or lands a punch that doesn't need one. None of them sound like they were written by someone trying to sound like a thought leader.

## LinkedIn posts
### 1. The AE who called it sales
*Mid-scene dialogue, confession without being precious, final line earns its punch.*
"Watch your mouth. Don't talk to me like that. I'm a designer." I said this to an account executive once. Half joking but not really. We'd just walked out of a half-day workshop I'd been running. A few blocks away from the client's building, he pulled me aside and said: "You know, you'd make a good salesperson." I bristled. "Every time you ask a question or propose something, I actually think you mean it. That's what would make you good at this." I've been chewing on that for years. Because I do mean it, that's the thing. I'm curious and I know this is an overplayed word right now. But when I'm in a room I actually want to figure out what's broken. No angle. Turns out, that's exactly what selling is — when it's done right. Not persuasion. Not pressure. Not the slow pivot from "just chatting" to "so, about our engagement model and retainer rates." More like: do you actually believe what you're saying? Do you care whether this helps them or not? I came up as a writer and designer, tactical work, making things. Someone else sold it — that's what I was comfortable with. Because sales felt like the opposite of the work I was doing. But the people who are weirdly good at getting clients aren't the ones with the best pitch. They're the ones who can't hide that they give a damn. The AE was right. I just didn't like the label he used.

### 2. The healthcare co-founders who didn't agree
*Dialogue does all the work — same nouns, different verbs. Closing question turns the story into a mirror.*
"We should do $99 MRIs," one co-founder blurted out. "It's the ultimate carrot. The growth would be through the roof." The other co-founder didn't lean in. He sat back and replied, very quietly: "I thought we were building this for a value-based care group. They don't care about that. They want to mitigate risk." The working session went quiet. We had to take a break. This was a few years ago. The team had already beta launched. Code in production, prioritized roadmap, a Storybook design system — the whole thing. But neither was wrong. They both knew the vertical. They'd both been in the room for every major decision for over a year. They'd just been using the same nouns — the patient, the physician, the cost of care — and assuming the verbs were the same too. They weren't. One was selling a price drop. The other was managing risk. Same product, two different companies. If your team hasn't finished the sentence out loud, in the same room, at the same time — you're not aligned. You're just co-existing until it gets expensive. When did your team last say it out loud?

### 3. The toolkit in the downloads folder
*Structural irony, not stated. Proof points give the confession teeth.*
Yesterday, I gave a talk at Wayne de Fremery's entrepreneurship class at Dominican University. My main points: get specific, find real humans, and talk to them. But this morning, I clicked on a lead gen toolkit promising 10 qualified leads and $50K in monthly revenue. I downloaded it, but I haven't opened it yet. The siren song of the "quick fix" is loud. I know better. With How This Works co, I've sold 10 clients. All 1st or 2nd-degree connections. No cold outreach, no ad spend, no email sequences. Every single one came through a real conversation with a real person with a real situation. The entrepreneur influencer content isn't evil. Some of it's genuinely good. But it's meant to feel like momentum. Clicking, downloading, commenting — that's motion. It's not the same as moving. Speed in the wrong direction is just a more expensive mistake. That toolkit's still in my downloads folder. Is there one you're ignoring?

### 4. Sneaky pitching
*Opens mid-scene, quotable line before the fold, ends with a principle not a CTA.*
The interview started like this: "Tell me about how you worked through this kind of marketing problem before." Sounds like discovery. Sounds like curiosity. Then, a few minutes in: "We're running a sign-up offer — want to take advantage?" I met three startup teams last week who said they were doing customer discovery. But they weren't. They were sneaky pitching. And the moment the person across the table realizes it, the conversation changes. They stop thinking, they stop sharing. They start being polite. Polite answers are the most dangerous data in startups. Because they feel like signal. They're not. Real discovery isn't about validating your idea. It's about understanding how people solve the problem today — even if that answer kills your product. You're not there to be heard. You're there to listen.

### 5. The Pied Piper post
*Specific metaphor, list does real damage, last line has teeth without explaining itself.*
I signed up for a new networking app yesterday. 15 min on a call with an AI. And it matched me with a couple of people. It did what was promised. Thanks. Then, in the very next email it asked if I knew someone from Seattle. A friend, actually. Did I think they'd be interested in signing up? Not sure. I'll ask. At the end, I asked the AI if I could have a transcript of our call and it sidestepped. And that's when I recognized the tune I was hearing. The Pied Piper didn't just play a song. He used it to extract what he wanted. The town paid him to solve the rat problem. Then, when crossed, he used the same trick to lure all the children away. Most "networking" tools work the same way. They solve isolation and loneliness. Then they quietly reach into your address book, your calendar, your trust network — and turn your relationships into their product. Alignable wanted my email address book. Lunch Club wanted access to my calendar. Boardy wanted to know who I know. With free offerings like this, you're not the customer. You're the prey. What I'm wondering: what problem is this actually solving, and for whom? Because sometimes the rat-catcher and the rats are working together.

### 6. PMF: "They like it" vs. "They need it"
*Opens with a data point that already unsettled the audience, delivers client story as proof, ends with a debatable distinction.*
43% of people who voted on my product-market fit poll last week chose "none of these — it's more complicated." Nearly half. And I think they're right. I worked with a founding team recently who'd hit Sean Ellis' threshold. 51% (more than 42%) of their users said they'd be "very disappointed" without the product. By this measure, they had PMF. But a year later, none of them renewed their subscription. The demand was real. But the fit wasn't. They'd found a vocal minority — people who loved the concept — and mistaken them for an actual market. Andreessen's pull wasn't really there. Olsen's "meaningfully better" wasn't true for the buyers who actually paid. Here's what none of the three frameworks ask: whose problem is this, exactly? Not the user who loves you. The one who can't function without you. That gap — between "they like it" and "they need it" — is where most early traction dies. What would you add to that?

### 11. The audiobook / virtual voice post
*Kid's instinct does all the arguing before any adult explanation.*
A week ago, my kid started the audiobook for a book they'd already read. And halfway through the first chapter, they stopped. "Something's off." They were right. I listened to it and looked it up. Audible calls it virtual voice. I've been thinking about this since my wife, Laura Clifton, finished recording Caput Mundi — a middle grade fantasy by B.R. Kang. The author insisted on a human narrator. Jim Dale's Hagrid lived in my head for years before I ever saw Robbie Coltrane. When I finally did, something clicked. Both performances had weight. A person decided who that character was. You can feel that. AI can do a lot, and some of it is useful. But "we'll handle the narration for your audiobook" is a subtraction dressed up as convenience. My wife Laura spent months recording it. I did the production and editing — 30+ hours, not counting the recording sessions. The author could have gone another way. She didn't. Links in the comments if you want to hear what that sounds like.

### 12. The em dash post
*Punchy, opinionated, self-aware, genuinely funny. Demonstrates voice without talking about voice.*
Apparently, em dashes are telltale signs that someone's used AI to "write something." As someone who might (or might not) over-use the longest of the dashes — used for breaks in thoughts where other punctuation might feel weird — I find this hilarious. It's true, some AI tools seem to overuse em dashes. But claiming they're exclusive to AI is like saying only Vampire Weekend cares about the oxford comma; it's just not true. Just ask Oakhurst Dairy about it, it cost them $5M in 2018. Writers have been using em dashes for effect — for emphasis — for transitions, for centuries... And in my 2019 Associated Press Stylebook, there's an entry on em dashes. It's legitimate punctuation that has standards, not an invention by AI chatbots. To everyone who thinks my use of em dashes (liberal or not) makes my writing sound "AI-generated" — I was doing this long before Claude/ChatGPT/Gemini existed, and I'll be doing it after the next AI trend comes and goes. Who else is on team em dash? Or do you prefer a regular dash? Or even, gasp, a semicolon and then you end the sentence altogether?

### 13. The calendar detox post
*Short, specific, anti-framework. The new phone analogy does all the heavy lifting.*
I'm a serial hand raiser. Sign me up and I'm there. Except next month. Starting week after next in Feb, barring client work, I'm clearing my calendar of group meetings. I'm looking for more whitespace. And then I'll add back what brings me business or personal value. It's the same logic I use with a new phone or new computer. I don't restore the backup. I add apps back when I actually need them, not because they're there. In this, I'm looking to build the muscle of saying no to make space for more yes in my work life.

## LinkedIn comments
### 14. The reply to Carmel on PMF
*Adds a layer rather than agreeing. Observer effect line is something you wouldn't find in a generic PMF comment.*
Love the 5 Whys connection — that kind of structured root cause work is exactly where discovery gets real. The thing I'd add: even those methods depend on who's asking the questions. The observer effect in customer interviews is real. People tell founders what they think founders want to hear. The closer you are to the product, the more you need someone in the room who doesn't have skin in the answer.

### 15. The sheep/shepherd AI strategy comment
*Metaphor comes from Skipper's own reaction. Final question is the one the original post dances around.*
The sheep/shepherd thing keeps coming back for me. Moving fast behind competent leadership is smart. Moving fast behind someone who doesn't know where they're going is how you go off a cliff. The crisis you're describing isn't really about pace — it's "AI to do what?" going unanswered while urgency accelerates. The teams I've seen get this right paused long enough to answer that question before they started running.

### 16. The sketching-as-cognition comment
*"Proof of cognition — an unfinished sentence you hand someone else to complete" does real work and couldn't come from a generic commenter.*
A sketch is proof of cognition — an unfinished sentence you hand someone else to complete. The fact that it's illegible to someone outside the conversation is the point. A hifi prototype answers questions. A sketch asks them. When AI builds the prototype, it answers the question you hadn't finished asking yet.

### 17. The "discovery theater" comment
*Engages from inside the conversation, not about it. Names the structural problem rather than restating the original point.*
The antidote to discovery theater isn't better research. The team needs to be in the room. When you route everything through one person's interpretation — even a good researcher — you get a long game of telephone. Engineers build for the findings document, not for what they actually heard. Getting the whole team into five interviews changes that. They argue about what they heard in real time. You don't need a meeting to align — you have shared experience.

### 18. The banned words list comment
*Adds a specific detail that only works if it's real. Does exactly what the original post advocates.*
What moved the needle for me was feeding about 100 of my own posts from the last year back through Claude, asking it to critique them, then using that to reshape my personal banned words list. The list keeps changing as the slop patterns evolve. Turns out I had a few of my own tells I didn't know about.

## Video scripts

### 19. Morning Story: Discovery vs. sales
*Trusts the scene to carry everything. "Polite answers are the most dangerous kind of data in startups" lands harder out loud.*

I met three startup teams last week who said they were doing customer discovery interviews. I thought, Great. It's catching on. But they weren't looking for customer stories. They were sneaky pitching. Here's how one of their interviews started: "Tell me about how you worked through this kind of marketing problem before." But a few minutes later, the real motive would come out: "We have a sign-up offer… want to take advantage?" That's not discovery. That's a sales call in sheep's clothing. And the problem is: the moment someone realizes they're being pitched, the conversation changes. The human you're talking to is now looking for the exits. You don't get the real story. You get polite answers. Real discovery interviews aren't about validating your idea. They're about understanding how people actually solve the problem today — even if the answer kills your product idea. And polite answers are the most dangerous kind of data in startups.
